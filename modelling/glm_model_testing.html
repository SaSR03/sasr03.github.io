Model testing

In this part you will learn about the following basic tasks:

  • Test whether a set of predictors, added together, significantly improves a regression model.

  • Compare two nested models using an F-test, a Wald test, and a likelihood ratio test, and see that these give the same conclusion.

  • Test whether a regression coefficient differs significantly between two groups (e.g., between men and women).

  • Combine the ideas of the previous chapters — dummy variables, control variables, and interactions — into a coherent model-building and model-testing strategy.

Comparing nested models

Two models are nested when one model (the smaller, or restricted, model) can be obtained from the other (the larger, or full, model) by setting one or more coefficients to zero. A common question is whether adding a set of predictors to a model significantly improves the fit, over and above the predictors already included.

There are three equivalent ways to test this:

  1. F-test for a set of coefficients. Fit the full model and test the joint null hypothesis that the added coefficients are all zero.
  2. Likelihood ratio (LR) test. Compare the log-likelihood of the restricted and the full model.
  3. Wald test. Test the joint null hypothesis for the added coefficients using their (co)variances from the full model only.

For linear regression estimated by ordinary least squares, all three approaches give (essentially) the same conclusion — asymptotically the F-test, LR test, and Wald test are equivalent. It is good practice to know more than one way to perform this test, since not every method extends equally easily to more complex models (e.g., generalized linear models), where the LR test and Wald test may differ.

Do not confuse a significant improvement in fit with a large improvement. A small increase in \(R^2\) can be statistically significant, especially in a large dataset, without being of much practical relevance. Always report and interpret the size of the change in \(R^2\), not just its p-value.

Comparing a coefficient across groups

Sometimes the question is not whether adding predictors improves the fit, but whether the effect of a predictor differs between groups (for instance, whether the effect of a pre-test score on a post-test score is the same for boys and girls). One way to answer this is to fit the model separately within each group and compare the coefficients formally, testing whether the two group-specific coefficients are equal. This is closely related to fitting a single model with an interaction between the predictor and a group indicator (as in the previous chapter); testing the interaction term is often the more direct route, but fitting separate models can be more transparent when many coefficients are allowed to differ across groups at once.

R

You will learn the R functions listed below for testing sets of coefficients and comparing nested models.

Function Description
anova() Compare two nested lm() models using an F-test
car::linearHypothesis() Wald test of one or more linear restrictions on the coefficients
logLik() Extract the log-likelihood of a fitted model
lmtest::lrtest() Likelihood ratio test comparing two nested models
update() Refit a model with a modified formula (e.g., adding or dropping terms)
broom::tidy() Tidy summary of model coefficients, useful for comparing across models

Examples:

Fit a restricted and a full model, and compare them with an F-test (this is the R equivalent of Stata’s nestreg):

library(tidyverse)
library(haven)

data <- read_dta("data/sesam.dta")

fit_restricted <- lm(postnumb ~ age + prenumb, data = data)
fit_full <- lm(postnumb ~ age + prenumb + prelet + prerelat + peabody, data = data)

anova(fit_restricted, fit_full)
summary(fit_full)

The anova() output reports the change in residual sum of squares, the F-statistic, degrees of freedom, and the p-value for the joint test that the added coefficients (prelet, prerelat, peabody) are all zero.

Test the same joint hypothesis with a Wald test, using only the full model:

library(car)

linearHypothesis(fit_full, c("prelet = 0", "prerelat = 0", "peabody = 0"))

Test the same joint hypothesis with a likelihood ratio test:

library(lmtest)

lrtest(fit_restricted, fit_full)

All three tests should lead to the same substantive conclusion.

Compare a coefficient between two groups by fitting the model separately within each group:

fit_male <- lm(postnumb ~ prenumb + age, data = data |> filter(sex == 1))
fit_female <- lm(postnumb ~ prenumb + age, data = data |> filter(sex == 2))

summary(fit_male)
summary(fit_female)

The more direct way to test whether a coefficient differs between two groups is to fit one model with an interaction between the predictor and the group indicator, and test the interaction term:

fit_interaction <- lm(postnumb ~ prenumb * factor(sex) + age, data = data)
summary(fit_interaction)

The coefficient for the interaction term prenumb:factor(sex) tests whether the effect of prenumb on postnumb differs between the two groups, while both models still control for age.

Improvement in fit: sesam

Use the dataset sesam.

  1. Fit a restricted model of postnumb on age and prenumb, and a full model that adds prelet, prerelat, and peabody. Compare the two models with an F-test (anova()). Does adding this set of three predictors significantly improve the fit? How large is the change in \(R^2\)? Is this change of much practical relevance?

  2. Repeat the same test using a Wald test (car::linearHypothesis()) on the full model. Do you reach the same conclusion as with the F-test?

  3. Repeat the same test again using a likelihood ratio test (lmtest::lrtest()) comparing the restricted and full models. Do all three methods agree?

  4. Which of the three added predictors (prelet, prerelat, peabody) is mainly responsible for the improvement in fit? Is this predictor’s effect of much substantive importance, even though it is statistically significant?

  5. Fit the model postnumb ~ prenumb + age separately for boys and for girls (sex == 1 and sex == 2). Compare the coefficient for prenumb between the two groups. Is the effect of prenumb on postnumb, controlling for age, significantly different for boys and girls? (Hint: refit the model as a single regression with an interaction between prenumb and sex, and test the interaction term, rather than comparing the two separate models informally.)

Building a model step by step: donations

Use the dataset donations. As before, treat the “miscellaneous” category of religion as missing and exclude these observations permanently. Construct propdon, the proportion of monthly income donated, and percdon, this proportion expressed as a percentage. Construct a dummy variable old for subjects older than 40.

  1. Fit a regression of percdon on old and religion (as a factor, with “none” as reference category). Is there evidence that older people (over 40) donate a larger share of their income than younger people, controlling for religion?

  2. Fit a regression of percdon on male and religion. Is there evidence that men donate a larger share of their income than women, controlling for religion? Comment on the strength of the evidence if the p-value is close to, but not below, 0.05.

  3. Using the model from the previous question, test whether religion as a whole contributes to explaining percdon, controlling for gender. Do this in at least two ways: (a) a joint test of all religion coefficients in the full model (e.g. car::linearHypothesis() or anova() on a single model with drop1()), and (b) comparing a restricted model without religion to the full model with religion, using anova(). Do the two approaches agree?

  4. Fit a regression of percdon on social integration (socint) alone. Is there evidence that donations increase with social integration? Now add religion to the model. What happens to the coefficient for socint? What does this tell you about the relationship between social integration, religion, and donations?

  5. Fit a restricted model of percdon on lifevent alone, and a full model that adds religion. Compare the two models with an F-test. Is there evidence that people who experienced a serious health problem (lifevent = 1) donate a larger share of their income than those who did not, controlling for religion?

  6. Fit a regression of percdon on inc alone, and then on inc together with religion. Is there evidence that donations increase with income, even after controlling for religion? Interpret the size of the effect.

  7. Construct the interaction between male and socint. Fit a regression of percdon on male, socint, and their interaction. Is the effect of social integration on donations different for men and women? Use margins-style predictions (e.g., compute the predicted slope of socint separately for each value of male from the fitted coefficients) to describe the effect separately for men and women.

  8. Fit a regression of percdon on male, years of education (edu), and their interaction. Is there evidence that the effect of education on donations differs between men and women?

  9. Fit a regression of percdon on male, lifevent, and their interaction. Is there evidence that the effect of having experienced a serious health problem on donations differs between men and women?
