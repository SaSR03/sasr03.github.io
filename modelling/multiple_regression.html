Multiple linear regression

Assignments

Predicting a post-test score

  1. Use the dataset sesam. We predict postnumb (a post-test score) from age and prenumb (a pre-test score).

    • Fit a regression of postnumb on age and prenumb. Interpret each coefficient, the intercept, and the \(R^2\). Is age a significant predictor once prenumb is controlled for?

    • Fit a regression of postnumb on prenumb alone. Compare the coefficient for prenumb and the \(R^2\) to the model that also includes age. What do you conclude?

Donations, religion, and income

Use the dataset donations, which contains information on voluntary donations.. Treat the “miscellaneous” category of religion as missing, and exclude the corresponding observations from the analyses below (it is too heterogeneous a group to interpret meaningfully).

  1. Test whether average donations differ between religious groups, controlling for income (inc). Fit a regression of donation on religion (as a factor) and inc. Which categories differ significantly from the reference category?

  2. Is there a difference in average donation between two specific religious categories that are not both compared to the reference category in the previous output? For example, is there a significant difference between rereformed and reformed?

  • One way of testing this is re-running the model with a different reference category. A better way is to use the function avg_comparisons() from package marginaleffects, which you have to install and load. You can obtain pairwise comparisons between all levels of a categorical (i.e. factor) variable with avg_comparisons(your_model_object, variables = list(your_variable_name = "pairwise")).

  1. Donations and income have been recorded in thousands. Look at the variable labels (glimpse(data_frame_name$variable_name)) to check in which time units they are recorded. Construct a new variable propdon, the proportion of monthly income donated. This will be outcome variable for the next questions. Report the mean and standard deviation of propdon. What do these values tell you?

  2. Fit a regression of propdon on religion and obtain pairwise comparisons between all categories. Between which categories is there evidence of a difference?

  3. Consider the following hypothesis: donations increase with income when controlling for religion, i.e., people gives a higher fraction of their income, the higher their income (in theoretical terms, charity is a “luxury good”). Fit a regression model to test this and interpret the results.

  4. Fit a regression of propdon on male, religion, and their interaction. Is there evidence that the effect of religion on donations differs between men and women?

  5. Fit a regression of propdon on inc, religion, and their interaction. Is there evidence that the effect of income on donations varies between religious denominations?

  6. Consider whether life events (lifevent) have a larger effect on charity among older people than among younger people. Beware: this formulation does not mean that we want you to dichotomize age. It is just an informal way of expressing that the effect of life events increases with age. Is there evidence that the effect of lifevent on propdon is larger for older people than for younger people?

  7. Test whether the effect of income on propdon varies with age. (For young people a smaller share of their income is discretionary due to mortgage, children, etc.)

  8. Test whether the effect of income on propdon varies by gender.

  9. Test whether the gender difference in income effects varies with religion (Note: this is actually a 3-way interaction).

Occupational prestige and education

Use the dataset gss91_extended. @e want to investigate whether the effect of education (educ) on occupational prestige (prestg80) is nonlinear.

  1. Make a scatterplot of prestg80 against educ. Overlay it with both a linear and a quadratic fitted line.
  • Tipo: for the quadratic line, use the formula y ~ x + I(x*x) in geom_smooth().

  1. Fit a regression model of prestg80 on educ, a squared term for educ, and the control variables race, region, and sex. Make sure that the control variables are interpreted as categorical variables, not numeric. Is the squared term statistically significant? What do you conclude about the shape of the relationship?

  2. Using the coefficients for educ and educ\(^2\), compute the turning point of the curve: the value of educ at which expected prestige is at its minimum or maximum. Is the curve U-shaped or inverted-U shaped? Check how many observations in the data actually fall at or below this turning point, and discuss whether the turning point should be interpreted, or whether it is more appropriate to describe the relationship only over the range where most of the data lie.

Age does not contribute in predicting postnumb (not significant). The #marginal correlation between age and postnumb is significant, but here the effect of age is conditional on that of prenumb: once you control for prenumb, age does not matter. If pretest score goes up by 1, posttest score significantly goes up by 0.78, irrespective of age. People aged 0 with a pretest score of 0 have a posttest score of 7.42; interpreting the constant is not meaningful here. About 45% of the variation in the post-test is explained by the pre-test and age.

14. Quadratic model with controls

To fit a quadratic line we can either create the squared term beforehand (i.e. create educ2 = educ*educ, then include both educ and educ2 in the equation) or specify the squared term with the syntax I(educ*educ). (Note that simply including educ*educ, as we would do for an interaction of different variables, does not work. Thus the need for I()).

fit_quad <- lm(
  prestg80 ~ educ + I(educ^2) + factor(race) + factor(region) + factor(sex),
  data = gss91
)

Only a small number of subjects have educ values at or below the turning point (about 12 out of roughly 1500). It is highly unlikely that prestige is actually higher with 1 or 2 years of education than# with 4 years; the model most plausibly applies for 4 and more years of education. We therefore abstain from interpreting the turning point itself, and conclude instead that, over the range where most of the data lie, prestige increases in education at an increasing rate.
