Multinomial logistic regression

In this part you will learn about the following basic tasks:

  • Recode a categorical outcome variable with several unordered categories.

  • Fit a multinomial logistic regression model, and choose a reference (base) category for the outcome.

  • Interpret coefficients as log relative-risk ratios, and exponentiate them into relative-risk ratios (RRRs).

  • Compute and interpret marginal effects on predicted probabilities for a multinomial outcome, including pairwise comparisons between response categories.

  • Test the assumption of Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA).

  • Include interactions between predictors, and test whether they improve model fit using a Wald test and a likelihood ratio test.

From binary to multinomial outcomes

Multinomial logistic regression extends logistic regression to an outcome variable with more than two unordered categories — for instance, which of several political parties a person intends to vote for. One category is chosen as the reference (base) category, and the model estimates one set of coefficients for each of the remaining categories, describing the (log) relative odds of being in that category rather than in the base category.

\[ \log \left( \frac{P(Y=j)}{P(Y = \text{base})} \right) = b_{0j} + b_{1j} X_1 + \dots + b_{kj} X_k \]

Which category is chosen as the base is arbitrary and does not affect the fit of the model, but it does affect which comparisons the coefficients directly represent. Choosing a substantively meaningful reference category (e.g., the largest group, or a natural “neutral” category) makes the output easier to interpret. It is good practice to fit the model with more than one choice of base category to make sure all pairwise comparisons of interest are covered, or to rely on marginal effects and pairwise comparisons (see below), which do not depend on the choice of base category.

Interpreting coefficients

As with binary logistic regression, there are several ways to interpret the coefficients of a multinomial model:

  1. Log relative-risk ratios. A one-unit increase in \(X\) is associated with a \(b_j\) change in the log of the relative risk of category \(j\) versus the base category, holding other predictors constant.

  2. Relative-risk ratios (RRR). Exponentiating a coefficient, \(\exp(b_j)\), gives the multiplicative change in the relative risk of category \(j\) versus the base category. An RRR above 1 means the relative risk of category \(j\) (versus the base) increases with \(X\); an RRR below 1 means it decreases.

  3. Marginal effects on predicted probabilities. As in binary logistic regression, marginal effects translate the model back to the probability scale: the average change in the predicted probability of each category of the outcome for a one-unit change in a predictor. Because a multinomial outcome has several categories, marginal effects are reported separately for each category, and their sum across all categories is necessarily zero (an increase in the probability of one category must come at the expense of the others). Pairwise comparisons between two specific response categories can also be computed directly.

The IIA assumption

Multinomial logistic regression relies on the assumption of Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA): the relative odds of choosing between any two categories should not depend on which other categories are also available. This is a strong and often unrealistic assumption. One common way to check it is the Hausman-McFadden style test: refit the model repeatedly, each time excluding all observations belonging to one specific outcome category, and test whether the coefficients for the remaining categories change. If they change substantially, IIA is violated for that comparison, and a model that relaxes the IIA assumption (e.g., a nested logit or multinomial probit model) may be more appropriate — such models are beyond the scope of this course.

R

You will learn the R functions listed below to fit and interpret multinomial logistic regression models.

Function Description
nnet::multinom() Fit a multinomial logistic regression model
relevel() Change the reference (base) category of the outcome or a predictor factor
summary() Display coefficients (log relative-risk ratios) and standard errors
exp(coef()) Convert coefficients to relative-risk ratios
car::linearHypothesis() Wald test of restrictions on the coefficients
lmtest::lrtest() Likelihood ratio test comparing two nested multinomial models
marginaleffects::avg_slopes() Average marginal effects on predicted probabilities, for each outcome category
marginaleffects::avg_slopes(..., hypothesis = "pairwise") Pairwise comparisons of marginal effects between outcome categories

Examples:

Recode a party-choice variable into a smaller number of unordered categories, and check the recoding:

library(tidyverse)
library(haven)

data <- read_dta("data/hin95_voting.dta")

data <- data |>
  mutate(
    party6 = case_match(
      party,
      1 ~ 2, 2 ~ 4, 3 ~ 3, 4 ~ 5, 5 ~ 1, 11 ~ 1, 6 ~ 6, 7 ~ 6, 8 ~ 6,
      .default = NA_real_
    )
  )

data |>
  count(party, party6)

Fit an empty (intercept-only) multinomial model, with a chosen base category, and inspect it again with a different base category:

library(nnet)

data <- data |>
  mutate(party6 = factor(party6))

fit_empty1 <- multinom(relevel(party6, ref = "1") ~ 1, data = data)
summary(fit_empty1)

fit_empty3 <- multinom(relevel(party6, ref = "3") ~ 1, data = data)
summary(fit_empty3)

Fit a model with predictors, and obtain relative-risk ratios:

data <- data |>
  mutate(
    fem = as.integer(sex == 2),
    rel4 = case_match(
      religion,
      1 ~ 1, 2 ~ 2, 3 ~ 3, 4 ~ 3, 8 ~ 4,
      .default = NA_real_
    ),
    rel4 = factor(rel4)
  )

fit <- multinom(relevel(party6, ref = "4") ~ education + rel4 + fem, data = data)
summary(fit)
exp(coef(fit))

Choose a different reference category for the categorical predictor rel4, and compare:

data <- data |>
  mutate(rel4 = relevel(rel4, ref = "3"))

fit_relevel <- multinom(relevel(party6, ref = "4") ~ education + rel4 + fem, data = data)
exp(coef(fit_relevel))

Average marginal effects on the predicted probability of each outcome category:

library(marginaleffects)

fit_me <- multinom(relevel(party6, ref = "1") ~ education + rel4 + fem, data = data)

avg_slopes(fit_me, variables = c("education", "fem"))

Pairwise comparisons of the marginal effect of a predictor between response categories:

avg_slopes(fit_me, variables = "rel4", by = "group")

Check IIA informally by refitting the model excluding each outcome category in turn, and comparing coefficients:

fit_full <- multinom(party6 ~ education + rel4 + fem, data = data)

fit_no1 <- multinom(party6 ~ education + rel4 + fem, data = data |> filter(party6 != "1"))
fit_no2 <- multinom(party6 ~ education + rel4 + fem, data = data |> filter(party6 != "2"))
# ... and so on for the remaining categories

coef(fit_full)
coef(fit_no1)
coef(fit_no2)

There is no single function that combines these separately-fitted models into one joint test of equal coefficients. In practice, informally comparing the coefficients and standard errors across the refitted models (as above) is the usual approach; substantial changes in a coefficient after excluding a category suggest that IIA does not hold for that comparison.

Include an interaction between two categorical predictors, and test whether it improves the fit:

fit_main <- multinom(relevel(party6, ref = "1") ~ edu4 + rel4, data = data)
fit_interaction <- multinom(relevel(party6, ref = "1") ~ edu4 * rel4, data = data)

lrtest(fit_main, fit_interaction)

Assignments

Use the dataset hin95_voting.

Recoding the outcome

  1. Recode the variable party (Dutch parties in the 1995 election) into a new variable party6 with six unordered categories: left, social democratic, social liberal, christian democratic, liberal, and strict religious. Categories that do not map onto one of these six groups should become missing. Check the recoding with a cross-tabulation of party against party6.

An empty model

  1. Fit a multinomial logistic regression of party6 on no predictors (an empty/intercept-only model), using category 1 as the base category. How do you interpret the intercepts?

  2. Refit the same empty model using category 3 as the base category instead. Is there evidence that people are more or less likely to be in category 4 than in category 3?

Interpreting coefficients

  1. Create a dummy variable fem for women. Recode religion into a new nominal variable rel4 with four categories. Fit a multinomial logistic regression of party6 on education, rel4, and fem, using category 4 as the base category for party6, and report relative-risk ratios (RRRs). Interpret the RRR for one unit of education for at least one outcome category.

  2. Refit the same model, but this time set category 3 of rel4 as the reference category for that predictor (instead of category 1). Compare the RRRs to the previous model. Why do the RRRs for rel4 change even though the model as a whole fits the data identically?

Marginal effects

  1. Fit a multinomial logistic regression of party6 on education, rel4 (with category 1 as reference), and fem, using category 1 as the base category for party6. Compute the average marginal effects of education and fem on the predicted probability of each outcome category.

  2. Refit with category 2 as the reference category for rel4. Compute the average marginal effect of rel4 on the predicted probabilities. Interpret one of these marginal effects.

  3. Compute pairwise comparisons of the marginal effect of rel4 between two specific response categories. Interpret one such pairwise comparison.

Checking IIA

  1. Fit the full multinomial model of party6 on education, rel4, and fem. Then refit the model six times, each time excluding all observations belonging to one category of party6. For each of the resulting models, compare the coefficients to those of the full model. For which outcome categories does the exclusion of another category appear to change the coefficients substantially, suggesting that IIA is violated? For which category does IIA appear to hold reasonably well?

  2. Based on your answer to the previous question, what do you conclude overall about whether the IIA assumption holds for this model of party choice?

Interactions and goodness of fit

  1. Recode education into an ordered variable edu4 with four categories. Fit a multinomial model of party6 on edu4 and rel4 (main effects only), using category 1 as the base category.

  2. Refit the model adding the interaction between edu4 and rel4. Test whether this interaction significantly improves the fit, using a likelihood ratio test comparing the two models.

  3. Is there evidence for an interaction between education and religion on party choice? What does — and does not — this tell you about whether education and religion interact in their effect on the predicted probabilities of voting for each party (as opposed to the relative-risk ratios)?

  4. Fit a multinomial model of party6 on education, rel4, and fem (main effects only), and a second model that adds all two-way and three-way interactions between these three predictors. Test whether the full set of interactions significantly improves the fit, using a likelihood ratio test.

13. What the significant interaction does and does not tell you

# There is evidence for an interaction between education and religion on
# the (log) relative-risk ratios describing party choice. This does NOT
# by itself imply that education and religion interact in their effect
# on the predicted *probabilities* of voting for each party -- a
# significant interaction in the relative-risk-ratio formulation does
# not automatically translate into a similarly interpretable interaction
# on the probability scale, because the mapping from relative risks to
# probabilities is nonlinear. If this interaction on the probability
# scale is itself of interest, a more detailed study (e.g. using
# marginal effects and their interactions) would be needed.
